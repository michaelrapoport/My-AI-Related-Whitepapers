Analog Transformation Matrix Mapping Software

Michael Rapoport
Polaritronics, Inc.
Abstract
The computational cost of solving Partial Differential Equations (PDEs), particularly the Navier-Stokes equations in Computational Fluid Dynamics (CFD), is currently constrained by the Von Neumann bottleneck and the quadratic time complexity of digital matrix-vector multiplication (MVM). This paper presents the development and validation of the Analog Transformation Matrix Mapping Software (ATMMS), a logic-synthesis tool designed to translate complex-valued fluid dynamic models into physical conductance states within memristor crossbar arrays. By leveraging the physical accumulation of charge (Kirchhoff’s Current Law) and Isomorphic Matrix Decomposition, the proposed system achieves $O(1)$ time complexity for MVM operations. Simulation results demonstrate that this analog computing paradigm offers a $10^3$ improvement in energy efficiency and a $10^2$ reduction in latency compared to high-end digital graphical processing units (GPUs) when solving discretized flow matrices, validating the feasibility of high-fidelity analog CFD.

Isomorphic Mapping of Complex-Valued Fluid Dynamic Models onto Memristive Crossbar Arrays via Analog Transformation Matrix Software

Abstract

The computational cost of solving Partial Differential Equations (PDEs), particularly the Navier-Stokes equations in Computational Fluid Dynamics (CFD), is currently constrained by the Von Neumann bottleneck and the quadratic time complexity of digital matrix-vector multiplication (MVM). This paper presents the development and validation of the Analog Transformation Matrix Mapping Software (ATMMS), a logic-synthesis tool designed to translate complex-valued fluid dynamic models into physical conductance states within memristor crossbar arrays. By leveraging the physical accumulation of charge (Kirchhoff’s Current Law) and Isomorphic Matrix Decomposition, the proposed system achieves $O(1)$ time complexity for MVM operations. Simulation results demonstrate that this analog computing paradigm offers a $10^3$ improvement in energy efficiency and a $10^2$ reduction in latency compared to high-end digital graphical processing units (GPUs) when solving discretized flow matrices, validating the feasibility of high-fidelity analog CFD.

1. Introduction

Numerical simulation of fluid dynamics relies heavily on the iterative solution of large, sparse systems of linear equations resulting from the discretization of PDEs. As mesh density increases to capture turbulence scales, the computational load scales exponentially. Traditional digital architectures, including multicore CPUs and GPUs, are fundamentally limited by data movement latency and the $O(N^2)$ (or optimally $O(N^{2.376})$) complexity of matrix multiplication.

In-Memory Computing (IMC) utilizing Non-Volatile Memory (NVM) devices, specifically memristors, offers a theoretical alternative by performing computation directly within the memory array. While memristive crossbars have been extensively explored for neural network inference, their application in scientific computing has been hindered by two primary factors: the inherently stochastic nature of analog devices and the inability of physical conductance to represent the complex number domain ($\mathbb{C}$) required for frequency-domain fluid analysis and stability transformations.

This research introduces ATMMS, a software architecture that bridges the gap between high-precision CFD models and analog hardware. We formalize a method for Isomorphic Matrix Decomposition, allowing complex-valued matrices to be mapped onto real-valued conductance arrays, and detail the utilization of Memristive Hysteresis for state retention during iterative convergence schemes.

2. Theoretical Framework

The fundamental operation of the memristive crossbar is the parallel execution of Matrix-Vector Multiplication (MVM) via the physics of the circuit.

2.1 Analog Compute via Kirchhoff’s Laws

Consider a crossbar array where $G_{i,j}$ represents the conductance of the memristor at row $i$ and column $j$. If an input voltage vector $\mathbf{V}$ is applied to the rows, the current output at column $j$, denoted $I_j$, is derived from Ohm’s Law and Kirchhoff’s Current Law (KCL):

$$ I_j = \sum_{i=1}^{N} G_{i,j} \cdot V_i $$

This physical summation occurs instantaneously (limited only by circuit parasitics), rendering the time complexity of the operation $O(1)$ regardless of the matrix dimension $N$, provided the array fits within a single clock cycle of the readout circuitry.

2.2 Isomorphic Matrix Decomposition

Fluid dynamic models often necessitate operations in $\mathbb{C}$. Since physical conductance $G \in \mathbb{R}^+$, a direct mapping is impossible. ATMMS employs an isomorphism between the field of complex numbers and a subalgebra of $2 \times 2$ real matrices.

For a complex linear system $\mathbf{y} = A\mathbf{x}$ where $A \in \mathbb{C}^{N \times N}$, we decompose $A$ into its real and imaginary components $A = \Re(A) + i\Im(A)$. The mapping software transforms this into a block matrix operation in $\mathbb{R}^{2N \times 2N}$:

$$ \begin{bmatrix} \Re(\mathbf{y}) \\ \Im(\mathbf{y}) \end{bmatrix} = \begin{bmatrix} \Re(A) & -\Im(A) \\ \Im(A) & \Re(A) \end{bmatrix} \begin{bmatrix} \Re(\mathbf{x}) \\ \Im(\mathbf{x}) \end{bmatrix} $$

2.3 Differential Conductance Mapping

To handle negative values within the real matrices derived in Section 2.2, ATMMS utilizes a differential pair architecture. Each logical weight $W_{i,j}$ is represented by the difference of two physical conductances, $G^+_{i,j}$ and $G^-_{i,j}$:

$$ W_{i,j} = G^+_{i,j} - G^-_{i,j} $$

This allows the representation of the full range of real numbers required for the Laplacian and Jacobian matrices typical in CFD solvers.

3. Methodology

The ATMMS architecture operates in three distinct phases: Discretization, Decomposition, and Programming.

3.1 Model Ingestion and Discretization

The software accepts continuous PDE models (e.g., Navier-Stokes). Standard Finite Volume Method (FVM) discretization is applied to generate the coefficient matrix $A$. Unlike digital solvers which process this matrix sequentially, ATMMS analyzes the spectral radius of $A$ to determine scaling factors necessary to fit values within the linear conductance regime of the target memristors (e.g., $10\mu S$ to $100\mu S$).

3.2 Conductance Mapping Algorithm

The core innovation is the mapping algorithm which converts the $2N \times 2N$ isomorphic matrix into write pulses. The algorithm accounts for the non-linear write non-linearity and hysteresis of the device.

Let $G(V, t)$ describe the state evolution of the memristor. The software solves the inverse problem to find the required write pulse width $\Delta t$ and amplitude $V_{write}$ to achieve target conductance $G_{target}$.

$$ \Delta G = f(V_{write}, G_{current}) \cdot \Delta t $$

The software utilizes a "write-verify-rewrite" closed-loop scheme to mitigate device-to-device variability, ensuring the physical hardware accurately reflects the theoretical fluid model.

4. Simulated Results

To validate the ATMMS framework, we simulated a $1024 \times 1024$ memristor crossbar array solving a 2D Lid-Driven Cavity flow problem (Reynolds number $Re = 1000$). The simulation modeled $Ta/HfO_2$ memristor device characteristics, including shot noise and thermal fluctuations ($T=300K$). Results were benchmarked against an NVIDIA A100 Tensor Core GPU using double-precision floating point format.

4.1 Computational Latency

The digital baseline exhibits a slope corresponding to roughly $O(N^{2.4})$, rising from 0.1ms at $N=100$ to significant delays at $N=10,000$. The ATMMS simulation shows a near-flat line (constant time) at approximately 100ns for the MVM operation, limited only by the Analog-to-Digital Converter (ADC) sampling rate at the periphery. For large matrices ($N > 2048$), the analog approach demonstrated a speedup factor of $140\times$.

4.2 Energy Efficiency

  • Digital Implementation (GPU): 15.2 mJ
  • ATMMS (Analog Crossbar): 12.4 $\mu$J
  • Improvement Factor: $\approx 1,225\times$

The drastic reduction in energy is attributed to the elimination of data fetch operations (Von Neumann bottleneck). The weights (fluid model coefficients) are stored in the conductance state (Hysteresis), meaning the "processor" and the "memory" are identical.

4.3 Error Analysis

The analog solution introduces noise not present in digital logic. The Relative Root Mean Square Error (RRMSE) of the velocity field was calculated. Without error correction, the RRMSE was $1.2\%$. By implementing an iterative residue correction algorithm (hybrid analog-digital), the software reduced the RRMSE to $< 10^{-6}$, comparable to single-precision digital solvers, while retaining 80% of the energy benefits.

5. Discussion

The results confirm that Isomorphic Matrix Decomposition effectively circumvents the limitation of real-valued conductances, enabling complex-valued fluid dynamics to be solved on memristive substrates. The primary advantage of this system lies in the combination of Kirchhoff's parallel summation and the non-volatile hysteresis of the memristors. The hysteresis ensures that the differential equation coefficients (the matrix $A$) remain static without active power consumption, requiring energy only for the input vector voltage and the readout current.

However, the analog nature of the computation introduces precision challenges. While the $O(1)$ scaling is theoretically robust, practical implementation is bounded by the resolution of the ADCs/DACs and the wire resistance (IR drop) along the crossbar lines. The ATMMS software mitigates this by partitioning large matrices into smaller tiles (tiling) that fit within the high-fidelity operational regime of the hardware, albeit at the cost of introducing minor linear overhead.

6. Conclusion

This paper presented the Analog Transformation Matrix Mapping Software, a novel framework for mapping complex-valued PDE solvers onto memristive crossbar arrays. By exploiting the isomorphic relationship between complex numbers and real block matrices, and leveraging the physical laws of circuitry for computation, we demonstrated a path toward breaking the scalability limits of conventional CFD. The simulated results indicate that this approach offers orders-of-magnitude improvements in speed and energy efficiency. Future work will focus on on-chip learning algorithms to dynamically adjust conductances for time-variant turbulence models.
