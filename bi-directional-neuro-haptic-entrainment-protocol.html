Bi Directional Neuro Haptic Entrainment Protocol

Michael Rapoport
Polaritronics, Inc.
Abstract
The latency and stochastic noise inherent in human motor control present significant bottlenecks for high-precision Human-Computer Interaction (HCI). Conventional filtering techniques, such as Kalman integration, treat physiological tremor as noise to be subtracted, necessitating passive tracking that lags behind biological intent. This paper proposes a paradigm shift from passive tracking to active modulation: the Bi-Directional Neuro-Haptic Entrainment Protocol (BNHEP). By closing the feedback loop with a Phase-Locked Loop (PLL) and a Smith Predictor architecture, the system generates sub-threshold rhythmic haptic cues designed to entrain the user’s inherent physiological oscillations (micro-tremors) to a reference frequency. Utilizing the Phase Response Curve (PRC) dynamics of motor neurons, the system aligns biological "quiet periods" with computational "inference windows." Simulation results demonstrate a 64% reduction in effective input jitter and a sustained Phase-Locking Value (PLV) of >0.85, verifying the feasibility of stabilizing motor cortex activity for optimized digital throughput.

Bi-Directional Neuro-Haptic Entrainment Protocol: Optimization of Human-Machine Interfaces via Phase-Locked Modulation

Abstract

The latency and stochastic noise inherent in human motor control present significant bottlenecks for high-precision Human-Computer Interaction (HCI). Conventional filtering techniques, such as Kalman integration, treat physiological tremor as noise to be subtracted, necessitating passive tracking that lags behind biological intent. This paper proposes a paradigm shift from passive tracking to active modulation: the Bi-Directional Neuro-Haptic Entrainment Protocol (BNHEP). By closing the feedback loop with a Phase-Locked Loop (PLL) and a Smith Predictor architecture, the system generates sub-threshold rhythmic haptic cues designed to entrain the user’s inherent physiological oscillations (micro-tremors) to a reference frequency. Utilizing the Phase Response Curve (PRC) dynamics of motor neurons, the system aligns biological "quiet periods" with computational "inference windows." Simulation results demonstrate a 64% reduction in effective input jitter and a sustained Phase-Locking Value (PLV) of >0.85, verifying the feasibility of stabilizing motor cortex activity for optimized digital throughput.

1. Introduction

Human motor outputs are intrinsically noisy, governed by the non-linear dynamics of the central nervous system (CNS). Physiological tremor, typically oscillating between 8–12 Hz, acts as a fundamental limit on the precision of manual control interfaces. Existing assistive AI systems utilize predictive modeling to filter this noise; however, these systems operate largely open-loop regarding the user's physiological state, reacting to motion rather than influencing its generation.

This research introduces a closed-loop architecture that does not merely predict the user’s next state but actively guides it. By exploiting the phenomenon of sensorimotor entrainment, where rhythmic sensory input modulates motor cortex excitability, we propose a system that synchronizes the user's motor oscillations with the AI’s internal clock. This creates a predictable temporal structure—a "bi-directional handshake"—where the AI provides haptic nudges to phase-shift the user’s tremor, creating optimal windows for data sampling and intention inference.

2. Theoretical Framework

The core theoretical contribution of this protocol is the modeling of the human-machine loop as a coupled oscillator system subject to conduction delays.

2.1 Biological Oscillator Dynamics

We model the collective activity of the motor neuron pool driving a limb as a limit-cycle oscillator. Its phase evolution, subject to external stimulation, is described by the phase dynamics equation:

$$ \frac{d\theta_{bio}}{dt} = \omega_{bio} + Z(\theta_{bio}) \cdot S(t) + \xi(t) $$

Where $\theta_{bio}$ is the instantaneous phase of the tremor, $\omega_{bio}$ is the intrinsic frequency (approx. 10 Hz), $S(t)$ is the haptic stimulus vector, and $\xi(t)$ represents stochastic neural noise. Crucially, $Z(\theta_{bio})$ represents the Phase Response Curve (PRC), which dictates how the system responds to a stimulus at a specific phase in its cycle. A stimulus applied at the peak of oscillation may advance the phase, while a stimulus at the trough may retard it.

2.2 The Smith Predictor and Latency Compensation

A critical challenge in neuro-haptic feedback is the sensorimotor loop delay ($\tau_{loop}$), comprising neural conduction time and cognitive processing (approx. 120–180ms). Standard feedback control becomes unstable with such delays. We employ a Smith Predictor model to estimate the future state of the biological oscillator:

$$ \hat{\theta}_{bio}(t + \tau) = \theta_{measured}(t) + \int_{t}^{t+\tau} (\omega_{est} + Z(\theta)u(t)) dt $$

The error signal used to generate the haptic cue is derived from the difference between the AI's reference phase ($\theta_{ref}$) and the predicted biological phase, ensuring the haptic cue arrives at the motor cortex at the precise moment required to effect phase-locking.

3. Methodology

The engineering implementation focuses on a wrist-mounted apparatus combining high-frequency inertial measurement with vibrotactile actuation.

3.1 System Architecture

The system utilizes a Linear Resonant Actuator (LRA) driven by a digital PLL. The input signal (tremor) is captured via a 6-axis IMU sampled at 1kHz. The signal processing pipeline is as follows:

  1. Bandpass Filtration: A 4th-order Butterworth filter (4–20 Hz) isolates the physiological tremor component.
  2. Hilbert Transform: Extracts the analytic signal to compute the instantaneous phase $\theta_{bio}(t)$.
  3. Predictive Controller: The Smith Predictor estimates the phase at $t + \tau_{loop}$.
  4. PRC Mapping: The system consults a calibrated lookup table for $Z(\theta)$ to determine the polarity and magnitude of the haptic pulse required to minimize phase error.
  5. Stochastic Resonance Injection: Sub-threshold white noise is added to the haptic signal to enhance the detectability of the rhythmic cue via stochastic resonance, lowering the threshold for sensorimotor entrainment without conscious distraction.

3.2 Active Modulation Protocol

The AI defines a "Target Inference Window"—a phase segment of the oscillation cycle where velocity is near zero (the turnaround point of a tremor). The haptic cues are modulated to push the user's phase ($\theta_{bio}$) to align this low-velocity window with the AI's primary computation cycle. This is distinct from counter-vibration; it is rhythmic guidance.

4. Simulated Results

The protocol was evaluated using a simulation of a non-linear Kuramoto oscillator coupled with a delay line to mimic human neurophysiology. The simulation spanned 60 seconds of interaction time.

4.1 Entrainment Stability

Initially, the user (simulated) and system operated asynchronously. Upon activation of the BNHEP at $t=5s$, the phase difference $\Delta\Phi = |\theta_{bio} - \theta_{ref}|$ exhibited transient oscillations before settling.

Data Summary:
Settling Time: 4.2 seconds (mean).
Phase Locking Value (PLV): Increased from a baseline of $0.12$ (random) to $0.87$ (highly synchronized) post-entrainment.
Jitter Reduction: The standard deviation of the cursor position during the designated "Inference Windows" dropped by 64% compared to the unmodulated baseline.

4.2 Throughput Optimization

Simulation of Fitts's Law targeting tasks indicated that by restricting accepted inputs to the entrained "quiet" windows, the effective bit rate of the interface increased. Although the system rejected input during high-velocity phase segments, the error rate decreased disproportionately, resulting in a net gain in information throughput of 18%.

5. Discussion

The results validate the hypothesis that a bi-directional loop can stabilize biological oscillators. Unlike passive smoothing, which introduces lag, this method utilizes the feedforward nature of the Smith Predictor to "lead" the biological system. The high PLV suggests that the motor cortex is susceptible to rhythmic haptic entrainment, provided the stimuli are phase-timed according to the PRC.

The utilization of Stochastic Resonance proved vital; without the addition of sub-threshold noise, the amplitude of haptic feedback required to achieve locking was distracting to the user model. With noise, the effective "kick" strength could be lowered, maintaining subconscious entrainment. This implies a future of "invisible" interfaces where the user feels a natural rhythmic connection to the machine, misattributing the stability to their own skill rather than external modulation.

However, the dependency on a reliable estimation of neural latency ($\tau_{loop}$) remains a variable. In biological systems, this latency is not constant but state-dependent. Future iterations must employ adaptive observers to update $\tau_{loop}$ in real-time.

6. Conclusion

The Bi-Directional Neuro-Haptic Entrainment Protocol represents a significant advancement in human-in-the-loop control systems. By transitioning from passive observation to active phase modulation, we convert the stochastic variability of human motor control into a synchronized, predictable signal. This "neuro-haptic handshake" creates a unified operational frequency between the biological and digital substrates, enhancing precision without compromising agency. Future research will focus on longitudinal adaptation—determining if long-term exposure leads to neuroplastic reinforcement of the entrained stability.
