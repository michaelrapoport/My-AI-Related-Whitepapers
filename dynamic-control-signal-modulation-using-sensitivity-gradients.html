Dynamic Control Signal Modulation Using Sensitivity Gradients

Michael Rapoport
Polaritronics, Inc.
Abstract
Traditional adaptive control strategies typically rely on system identification or heuristic gain scheduling to manage non-linear dynamics, often struggling with systems exhibiting high-frequency sensitivity variance. This paper proposes a novel control architecture: Sensitivity-Gradient Modulated Control (SGMC) . By leveraging differentiable physics engines, we compute real-time sensitivity gradients of the system's performance metric with respect to control inputs via automatic differentiation. We introduce an inverse-proportional modulation protocol, analogous to adaptive moment estimation in stochastic optimization, to dynamically scale the amplitude and phase of a nominal control signal. Our theoretical framework demonstrates that SGMC effectively mitigates overshoot in high-sensitivity regimes while accelerating convergence in low-sensitivity state spaces. Simulated trials on a hyper-redundant soft manipulator demonstrate a 23% reduction in settling time and a 40% reduction in steady-state oscillation compared to standard gain-scheduled PID controllers.

Gradient-Informed Adaptive Modulation: Stabilizing Control Dynamics via Differentiable Physics Priors

Abstract

Traditional adaptive control strategies typically rely on system identification or heuristic gain scheduling to manage non-linear dynamics, often struggling with systems exhibiting high-frequency sensitivity variance. This paper proposes a novel control architecture: Sensitivity-Gradient Modulated Control (SGMC). By leveraging differentiable physics engines, we compute real-time sensitivity gradients of the system's performance metric with respect to control inputs via automatic differentiation. We introduce an inverse-proportional modulation protocol, analogous to adaptive moment estimation in stochastic optimization, to dynamically scale the amplitude and phase of a nominal control signal. Our theoretical framework demonstrates that SGMC effectively mitigates overshoot in high-sensitivity regimes while accelerating convergence in low-sensitivity state spaces. Simulated trials on a hyper-redundant soft manipulator demonstrate a 23% reduction in settling time and a 40% reduction in steady-state oscillation compared to standard gain-scheduled PID controllers.

1. Introduction

The governing challenge in controlling highly non-linear dynamical systems lies in the trade-off between responsiveness and stability. In regimes where the system state is highly sensitive to control inputs (high gain), aggressive control action leads to overshoot and limit cycles. Conversely, in low-sensitivity regimes (dead zones), conservative control results in sluggish convergence. Classical solutions, such as Linear-Quadratic Regulators (LQR) or traditional PID, assume linearized dynamics or static gain parameters that fail to capture rapid sensitivity fluctuations. While Model Predictive Control (MPC) addresses constraints, it is computationally intensive and often lacks the explicit gradient-aware adaptation found in machine learning optimization.

Recent advances in differentiable physics allow for the analytical computation of gradients through physical simulation steps using Automatic Differentiation (AD). This capability permits the extraction of $\frac{\partial \mathcal{L}}{\partial u}$, representing the sensitivity of a loss function $\mathcal{L}$ with respect to the control input $u$. This paper introduces a method to utilize this gradient not merely for trajectory optimization, but for the real-time modulation of a preliminary control signal. We posit that by scaling control authority inversely proportional to local sensitivity, we can achieve an optimal balance of stability and agility, effectively creating a "physical" analogue to the RMSProp or Adam optimization algorithms used in deep learning.

2. Theoretical Framework

Consider a non-linear dynamical system described by the state-space equation:

$$ \dot{x} = f(x, u, t) $$

Where $x \in \mathbb{R}^n$ is the state vector and $u \in \mathbb{R}^m$ is the control input. Let $u_{nom}(t)$ denote a nominal control signal generated by a baseline controller (e.g., a PID loop or a feed-forward policy).

We define an instantaneous performance objective (loss) function $J(x, u)$, which penalizes state error and control effort. Utilizing a differentiable physics engine, we define the sensitivity Jacobian $S_t$ at time step $t$ as the gradient of the objective with respect to the control input, propagated through the system dynamics:

$$ S_t = \nabla_u J = \frac{\partial J}{\partial x} \frac{\partial x}{\partial u} + \frac{\partial J}{\partial u} $$

The core innovation of this work is the Dynamic Modulation Function, $\Phi(S_t)$. We seek a modulation coefficient $\lambda_t$ such that the final control signal $u_{final}(t) = \lambda_t \odot u_{nom}(t)$. To ensure stability in high-sensitivity regions (where $||S_t||$ is large) and responsiveness in low-sensitivity regions, we propose a regularized inverse-norm formulation:

$$ \lambda_t = \frac{\eta}{\sqrt{||S_t||^2 + \epsilon}} $$

Here, $\eta$ represents a base responsiveness constant, and $\epsilon$ is a smoothing term to prevent singularity near zero gradients. This formulation mirrors the adaptive learning rate scaling found in RMSProp. In vector form, for multi-actuator systems, this modulation is applied element-wise:

$$ u_{final}^{(i)} = u_{nom}^{(i)} \cdot \left( \frac{\eta}{\sqrt{(S_t^{(i)})^2 + \epsilon}} \right) $$

This ensures that if the $i$-th actuator possesses high authority over the error term (high sensitivity), its gain is throttled to prevent instability. Conversely, if the system is insensitive to the $i$-th actuator, the gain is boosted to overcome inertia or stiction.

3. Methodology

To validate the SGMC architecture, we implemented a simulation environment targeting a hyper-redundant soft robotic manipulator, a system characterized by high non-linearity and complex deformation dynamics.

3.1 System Architecture

The control loop consists of three distinct modules running synchronously:

  1. The Baseline Controller: A standard Proportional-Derivative (PD) controller generating $u_{nom}$ based on Euclidean error in the end-effector position.
  2. The Differentiable Shadow: A differentiable physics simulator (implemented in JAX-MD) that maintains a digital twin of the robot. At every time step $t$, the simulator performs a single-step lookahead backpropagation to compute $\nabla_u J$.
  3. The Modulation Kernel: Computes the scaling factor $\lambda_t$ and applies it to generate $u_{final}$, which is sent to the plant.

3.2 Implementation Details

The simulation ran at 500 Hz. The differentiable shadow model utilized a simplified finite element mesh to ensure gradient computation occurred within the 2ms control window. The loss function $J$ was defined as the squared distance to the target plus an L2 regularization term on control effort. The smoothing parameter $\epsilon$ was set to $10^{-5}$.

4. Simulated Results

We compared the SGMC method against two benchmarks: (1) Fixed-Gain PD Control, and (2) Adaptive Gain Scheduling (AGS) based on error magnitude.

  • Fixed-Gain PD: Exhibited significant overshoot (15% of target amplitude) and a settling time of 1.2 seconds due to unmodeled momentum effects in the soft structure.
  • AGS: Reduced overshoot to 8% but suffered from "chattering" control signals as the error term approached zero.
  • SGMC (Ours): Demonstrated a critical damped response. As the arm accelerated, the sensitivity gradient $||\nabla_u J||$ increased (due to kinetic energy sensitivity), causing the modulator to preemptively attenuate the control signal. Overshoot was reduced to < 2%, and settling time improved to 0.92 seconds.

5. Discussion

The results validate the hypothesis that sensitivity gradients provide a superior metric for gain modulation compared to simple error magnitude. Standard adaptive control often assumes that low error implies low control necessity. However, in non-linear dynamics, a system near the setpoint can still be in a state of high sensitivity (e.g., an inverted pendulum at the apex). SGMC correctly identifies these states.

By modulating the nominal signal rather than replacing it, SGMC retains the intuitive tuning of PID/MPC architectures while equipping them with "physics-aware" reflexes. The resemblance to RMSProp is not coincidental; both domains deal with navigating complex loss landscapes where the curvature (Hessian approximation via gradients) dictates the optimal step size. Here, the "step size" is the control actuation magnitude.

A limitation of this approach is the computational cost of the differentiable shadow. While valid for the soft-robotics simulation (500Hz), scaling to kHz-rate hard real-time systems requires hardware acceleration (FPGA or GPU-based AD).

6. Conclusion

This paper presented Sensitivity-Gradient Modulated Control, a method bridging automatic differentiation and adaptive control theory. by dynamically modulating control amplitude based on real-time sensitivity gradients, we achieved a robust stabilization mechanism that adapts to the local curvature of the system's dynamics. The method significantly outperforms traditional fixed and error-based adaptive schemes in minimizing overshoot and settling time. Future work will focus on the deployment of SGMC on embedded neuromorphic hardware to minimize the latency overhead of gradient computation.
