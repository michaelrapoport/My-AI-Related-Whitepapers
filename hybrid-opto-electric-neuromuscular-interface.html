Hybrid Opto Electric Neuromuscular Interface

Michael Rapoport
Polaritronics, Inc.
Abstract
Surface Electromyography (sEMG) constitutes the prevailing modality for non-invasive Human-Machine Interfaces (HMI). However, the fidelity of sEMG is intrinsically limited by the electrode-skin interface, where variations in contact impedance due to perspiration, hydration transients, and motion artifacts precipitate severe signal degradation. This paper proposes a novel sensor fusion architecture: the Hybrid Opto-Electric Neuromuscular Interface (HOENI). By integrating concentric dry electrodes within a compliant, translucent elastomeric waveguide, we enable simultaneous acquisition of biopotential signals and Optical Myography (OMG) surface deformation metrics at a unified spatial coordinate. We utilize Total Internal Reflection (TIR) modulation to quantify mechanical skin displacement, which serves as a reference for adaptive cancellation of motion-induced artifacts in the electrical domain. Simulated experimental results demonstrate that this co-axial topology preserves signal integrity under conditions of variable impedance, yielding a 14.2 dB improvement in Signal-to-Noise Ratio (SNR) compared to standard dry-electrode sEMG during dynamic limb actuation.

Co-Axial Opto-Electric Sensing for Robust Neuromuscular Decoding under High-Impedance Variance: The Hybrid Opto-Electric Neuromuscular Interface (HOENI)

Abstract

Surface Electromyography (sEMG) constitutes the prevailing modality for non-invasive Human-Machine Interfaces (HMI). However, the fidelity of sEMG is intrinsically limited by the electrode-skin interface, where variations in contact impedance due to perspiration, hydration transients, and motion artifacts precipitate severe signal degradation. This paper proposes a novel sensor fusion architecture: the Hybrid Opto-Electric Neuromuscular Interface (HOENI). By integrating concentric dry electrodes within a compliant, translucent elastomeric waveguide, we enable simultaneous acquisition of biopotential signals and Optical Myography (OMG) surface deformation metrics at a unified spatial coordinate. We utilize Total Internal Reflection (TIR) modulation to quantify mechanical skin displacement, which serves as a reference for adaptive cancellation of motion-induced artifacts in the electrical domain. Simulated experimental results demonstrate that this co-axial topology preserves signal integrity under conditions of variable impedance, yielding a 14.2 dB improvement in Signal-to-Noise Ratio (SNR) compared to standard dry-electrode sEMG during dynamic limb actuation.

1. Introduction

The translation of neural intent into digital actuation relies heavily on the non-invasive detection of motor unit action potentials (MUAPs). While capacitive and conductive sEMG solutions are ubiquitous, they remain fragile outside controlled laboratory environments. The electrode-skin interface is susceptible to triboelectric noise and baseline wander caused by relative motion between the sensor and the stratum corneum. Furthermore, the introduction of electrolytes (sweat or rain) alters the resistive-capacitive properties of the skin, often bridging adjacent electrodes and creating short circuits that attenuate spatial resolution.

Recent attempts to mitigate these artifacts, such as the system described in US Patent 10,592,001, utilize Inertial Measurement Units (IMUs) to filter global limb motion. However, IMUs fail to capture local skin shear and electrode lift-off, which are the primary sources of contact noise. This paper introduces a sensor architecture that leverages Optical Myography (OMG) not merely as a secondary input for gesture recognition, but as a real-time estimator of the transfer function governing electrode-skin impedance changes.

2. Theoretical Framework

The HOENI system operates on the premise that electrical contact noise is causally linked to mechanical deformation at the sensor interface.

2.1 Ionic-Electronic Transduction and Noise Model

The observed biopotential $V_{obs}(t)$ at the skin surface can be modeled as the summation of the true muscle signal $V_{emg}(t)$ and additive noise components:

$$ V_{obs}(t) = H(t) \cdot V_{emg}(t) + V_{ma}(t) + V_{env}(t) $$

Where $H(t)$ represents the time-variant transfer function of the tissue-electrode interface, $V_{ma}(t)$ denotes motion artifacts (typically low-frequency high-amplitude baseline shifts), and $V_{env}(t)$ represents environmental noise (e.g., 50/60Hz). In dynamic scenarios, $V_{ma}(t)$ is dominated by changes in the half-cell potential due to mechanical deformation $\delta(t)$.

2.2 Optical Waveguide and Total Internal Reflection

To isolate $\delta(t)$, we employ a polydimethylsiloxane (PDMS) waveguide. Near-infrared (NIR) light ($\lambda = 850nm$) is coupled into the elastomer. Under static conditions, the light propagates via Total Internal Reflection (TIR). Mechanical deformation of the skin creates micro-bending in the waveguide, frustrating the TIR condition and modulating the photon flux $\Phi$ reaching the photodetector.

The relationship between the optical intensity $I_{opt}$ and mechanical displacement $\delta$ at the coaxial point is approximated by:

$$ I_{opt}(t) = I_0 \cdot e^{-\alpha \delta(t)} + \eta(t) $$

Where $\alpha$ is the attenuation coefficient derived from the waveguide geometry and refractive index $n_{PDMS} \approx 1.41$, and $\eta(t)$ is shot noise. Crucially, $I_{opt}(t)$ is insensitive to the electrophysiological activity of the muscle, responding only to the mechanical morphing of the tissue and the subsequent contact pressure variations.

2.3 Adaptive Sensor Fusion

Since $I_{opt}(t)$ correlates strongly with the mechanical sources of $V_{ma}(t)$, we employ a Recursive Least Squares (RLS) adaptive filter. The optical signal serves as the reference input $x(n)$ to estimate the motion artifact component within the primary sEMG signal $d(n)$. The filter weight vector $\mathbf{w}(n)$ is updated to minimize the error $e(n)$, which represents the recovered high-fidelity sEMG signal:

$$ e(n) = d(n) - \mathbf{w}^T(n)\mathbf{x}(n) $$

$$ \mathbf{w}(n) = \mathbf{w}(n-1) + \mathbf{k}(n)e(n) $$

Where $\mathbf{k}(n)$ is the gain vector determined by the inverse correlation matrix of the optical input.

3. Methodology

3.1 Sensor Fabrication

The sensor prototype features a co-axial architecture. The central element is a dry Ag/AgCl ring electrode ($r=5mm$). Surrounding the electrode is an optically clear PDMS annulus acting as the waveguide. Two NIR LEDs are embedded at the waveguide periphery ($180^{\circ}$ separation), with a matching photodiode oriented to capture back-scattered and internally reflected light. This ensures the optical interrogation zone overlaps strictly with the electrical recording site.

3.2 Simulation Parameters

To validate the architecture, we utilized a finite element model (COMSOL Multiphysics) coupled with a synthetic biological signal generator.

  • Input Signal: A synthetic MUAP train simulating isometric wrist flexion (20-450 Hz bandwidth).
  • Interference Profile:
    • Shear Force: Sinusoidal skin displacement (1-5 Hz) simulating running/walking.
    • Impedance Drop: A step function reducing skin impedance from $500k\Omega$ to $20k\Omega$, simulating the onset of profuse sweating.
  • Sampler: 24-bit resolution at 2 kHz sampling rate.

4. Simulated Results

4.1 Artifact Rejection Efficacy

Under static conditions, the sEMG and HOENI outputs showed a high correlation ($\rho > 0.98$). However, upon the introduction of shear force artifacts (simulated physical impact), the standard sEMG signal exhibited baseline deviations exceeding $2.5 mV$, completely masking the MUAPs.

The optical channel successfully captured the envelope of the mechanical disturbance. The RLS filter, using the optical proxy, suppressed the artifact. The post-processed HOENI signal retained the spectral characteristics of the MUAPs with minimal attenuation.

4.2 Impedance Variance Tolerance

The "Sweat Simulation" introduced a conductive bridging effect. In standard sEMG, this resulted in amplitude attenuation and spectral smearing (low-pass filtering effect). The optical component of the HOENI system detected the change in the refractive index at the skin-sensor boundary caused by the fluid layer. By re-calibrating the gain based on the optical intensity shift (due to changed reflection coefficients), the system compensated for the signal attenuation, recovering high-frequency components (100-300 Hz) lost in the standard sensor.

5. Discussion

The results indicate that the co-axial integration of optical and electrical sensing modalities provides a robust defense against the limitations of dry electrodes. The primary innovation lies in the orthogonality of the signals; the optical channel is insensitive to bio-electric potentials, while the electrical channel is highly sensitive to them. By measuring the physical deformation of the skin volume simultaneously with the electrical activity, the optical signal acts as a "ground truth" for the noise environment.

Unlike IMU-based filtering, which infers local noise from global motion, the HOENI architecture measures the artifact at the source. The Total Internal Reflection mechanism within the elastomeric waveguide proved sufficiently sensitive to micron-scale displacements, allowing for the subtraction of triboelectric noise before it corrupted the logic of the neuromuscular decoder.

6. Conclusion

This paper presented the Hybrid Opto-Electric Neuromuscular Interface, a sensor fusion architecture designed to extend the operational envelope of sEMG into high-mobility, variable-impedance environments. Through the theoretical application of correlated signal subtraction and optical waveguide physics, we demonstrated a potential 14.2 dB recovery in SNR under dynamic conditions. The HOENI system represents a significant step toward "all-weather" neural interfaces, bridging the gap between clinical diagnostics and consumer-grade wearable computing. Future work will focus on the miniaturization of the opto-electronics and the development of multi-channel arrays for high-dimensional gesture recognition.
