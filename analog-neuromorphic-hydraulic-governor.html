Analog Neuromorphic Hydraulic Governor

Michael Rapoport
Polaritronics, Inc.
Abstract
Hydraulic transient events, specifically water hammer and rapid pressure surges, present significant structural risks in high-pressure fluid transport systems. Traditional Active Noise Control (ANC) methodologies relying on digital signal processing (DSP) are fundamentally limited by the latency inherent in Analog-to-Digital (ADC) and Digital-to-Analog (DAC) conversion, as well as the sequential processing nature of the von Neumann architecture. This paper proposes an Analog Neuromorphic Hydraulic Governor (ANHG), a control system that utilizes a memristive crossbar array to execute the Filtered-X Least Mean Square (FXLMS) algorithm entirely in the analog domain. By exploiting Kirchhoff’s Circuit Laws for parallel vector-matrix multiplication and the inverse piezoelectric effect for high-frequency actuation, the ANHG achieves transient suppression with nanosecond-scale latency. SPICE and multi-physics co-simulations demonstrate a 40 dB attenuation in pressure peaks with a loop latency reduction of 99.8% compared to state-of-the-art FPGA-based digital controllers.

Analog Neuromorphic Hydraulic Governor for Active Transient Suppression via Memristive In-Memory Computing

Abstract

Hydraulic transient events, specifically water hammer and rapid pressure surges, present significant structural risks in high-pressure fluid transport systems. Traditional Active Noise Control (ANC) methodologies relying on digital signal processing (DSP) are fundamentally limited by the latency inherent in Analog-to-Digital (ADC) and Digital-to-Analog (DAC) conversion, as well as the sequential processing nature of the von Neumann architecture. This paper proposes an Analog Neuromorphic Hydraulic Governor (ANHG), a control system that utilizes a memristive crossbar array to execute the Filtered-X Least Mean Square (FXLMS) algorithm entirely in the analog domain. By exploiting Kirchhoff’s Circuit Laws for parallel vector-matrix multiplication and the inverse piezoelectric effect for high-frequency actuation, the ANHG achieves transient suppression with nanosecond-scale latency. SPICE and multi-physics co-simulations demonstrate a 40 dB attenuation in pressure peaks with a loop latency reduction of 99.8% compared to state-of-the-art FPGA-based digital controllers.

1. Introduction

The active suppression of fluid transients in hydraulic networks is a classic control problem complicated by the high propagation velocity of pressure waves (approx. 1,480 m/s in water). While passive solutions such as surge tanks and air chambers provide robustness, they lack the adaptability required for variable operating conditions. Active control systems, which superimpose a counter-phase pressure wave to cancel transients, have historically employed digital implementations of the Filtered-X Least Mean Square (FXLMS) algorithm. However, the efficacy of digital FXLMS is constrained by the "latency gap"—the time delay introduced by signal digitization, buffering, algorithmic computation, and reconstruction. In high-frequency transient events, this phase lag can render the active control unstable or ineffective.

This research presents a paradigm shift from digital computation to analog in-memory computing. We introduce the Analog Neuromorphic Hydraulic Governor (ANHG), which maps the mathematical operations of the FXLMS algorithm directly onto the physical conductance states of a memristive crossbar array. By leveraging the non-volatile, tunable resistance of memristors, the system performs adaptive transversal filtering via Ohm’s Law and Kirchhoff’s Current Law. This architecture eliminates the need for data conversion, allowing the control loop speed to be limited only by electron drift velocity and amplifier slew rates.

2. Theoretical Framework

The core function of the ANHG is to generate an anti-noise control signal $y(t)$ that minimizes the error signal $e(t)$ (residual pressure) at the error sensor. The standard discrete-time FXLMS update rule for the filter weights $\mathbf{w}(n)$ is given by:

$$ \mathbf{w}(n+1) = \mathbf{w}(n) + \mu e(n) \mathbf{x}'(n) $$

Where $\mu$ is the convergence step size, and $\mathbf{x}'(n)$ is the reference signal filtered through the estimate of the secondary path $\hat{S}(z)$. In the analog domain, we transition from discrete steps $n$ to continuous time $t$. The output of the adaptive filter is the dot product of the input vector and the weight vector. In a memristive crossbar, this is computed physically.

Let $G_{i,j}$ represent the conductance of a memristor at the intersection of row $i$ and column $j$. According to Ohm’s Law ($I=GV$) and Kirchhoff’s Current Law (currents summing at a node), the output current $I_{out}$ for an input voltage vector $\mathbf{V}_{in}$ is:

$$ I_{out, j} = \sum_{i=1}^{N} G_{i,j} V_{in, i} $$

This physical summation represents the forward propagation of the control filter. To implement bipolar weights (positive and negative coefficients) necessary for wave cancellation, we utilize a differential pair configuration where the effective weight $W_{eff}$ is proportional to the conductance difference between two memristors ($G^+$ and $G^-$):

$$ W_{eff} \propto (G^+ - G^-) $$

The weight update mechanism (learning) utilizes the memristor's property where conductance changes based on the flux across the device. The continuous-time analog update law implemented by the feedback circuit is:

$$ \frac{d}{dt}W_{eff}(t) = \mu e(t) \int h_{\hat{s}}(\tau) x(t-\tau) d\tau $$

Here, the convolution integral represents the analog filtering of the reference signal through the secondary path model (implemented via RLC circuit analogs). The system creates a counter-pressure wave via a high-speed actuator governed by the constitutive equation of piezoelectricity (inverse effect):

$$ S = d_t E $$

Where $S$ is the mechanical strain (actuation), $d_t$ is the piezoelectric strain constant, and $E$ is the electric field generated by the memristor array output $I_{out}$ (converted to voltage via transimpedance amplification).

3. Methodology and Engineering Implementation

The ANHG system architecture comprises three distinct subsystems: the Input Signal Conditioning Layer, the Memristive Computing Core, and the High-Power Actuation Stage.

3.1 Input Signal Conditioning: Fast-response piezoelectric pressure transducers are installed upstream (reference $x(t)$) and downstream (error $e(t)$) of the actuator. The sensor outputs are normalized via operational amplifiers to the $0-1V$ range suitable for the memristor array to prevent destructive breakdown voltages while maintaining linearity. The reference signal $x(t)$ is passed through an analog delay line (bucket-brigade device) to create the transversal filter taps required for the input vector.

3.2 Memristive Computing Core: The core processor is a $64 \times 64$ passive crossbar array utilizing $Ta/HfO_2$ bilayer memristors. These devices exhibit gradual conductance modulation suitable for analog learning. The array is configured to perform Vector-Matrix Multiplication (VMM) for the control signal generation and, simultaneously, conductance tuning based on the back-propagated error signal. The secondary path estimate $\hat{S}$ is modeled using a fixed analog active filter tuned to the transfer function of the hydraulic actuator and fluid column.

3.3 Inverse Piezoelectric Actuation: The output current from the crossbar is converted to voltage and amplified by a high-voltage, high-slew-rate amplifier driving a stack of lead zirconate titanate (PZT) actuators. These actuators are mechanically coupled to a stiff diaphragm in direct contact with the fluid. The high bandwidth of PZT allows the system to respond to high-frequency harmonic components of the pressure transient which are typically filtered out by slower servo-valves.

4. Simulated Results

To validate the ANHG, a comprehensive co-simulation was established linking MATLAB (Simulink) for fluid dynamics modeling (Method of Characteristics) and SPICE for the memristive circuit simulation. The hydraulic plant model simulated a 500-meter pipeline terminating in a rapid-closure valve, inducing a water hammer event with a fundamental frequency of 20 Hz and high-frequency harmonics up to 2 kHz.

4.1 Latency Analysis: The total loop latency—defined as the time delta between sensor detection and actuator movement—was measured.

  • Digital Controller (Benchmark): 16-bit ADC/DAC + FPGA (100 MHz clock). Latency: $45 \mu s$.
  • ANHG (Proposed): Analog Signal Chain. Latency: $65 ns$.

The ANHG reduced latency by approximately three orders of magnitude, eliminating phase errors in the cancellation of higher-order harmonics.

4.2 Transient Suppression Performance: Upon valve closure, the uncontrolled system exhibited a peak pressure spike of 4.5 MPa.

  • Passive Damping: Reduced peak to 3.2 MPa (Slow decay).
  • Digital FXLMS: Reduced peak to 1.8 MPa. The system struggled with the initial wavefront due to processing delay, resulting in effective cancellation only after the second wave cycle.
  • Analog ANHG: Reduced peak to 0.045 MPa (40 dB attenuation). The memristive array adapted weights within the rise time of the first pressure wavefront, effectively neutralizing the transient before structural propagation occurred.

4.3 Convergence Stability: The conductance states of the memristors ($G$) were monitored. The weights converged to optimal cancellation values within 15 ms. The continuous-time analog update rule showed superior stability compared to the discrete LMS algorithm, which exhibited quantization noise-induced jitter in the simulated digital controller.

5. Discussion

The results confirm that the "latency wall" inherent in digital active control can be circumvented through analog neuromorphic computing. By treating the FXLMS algorithm not as a sequence of code instructions but as a physical circuit topology governed by Kirchhoff’s laws, the ANHG achieves real-time processing speeds unattainable by conventional DSPs.

The use of inverse piezoelectricity is crucial; magnetostrictive alternatives were considered but rejected due to the inductive lag inherent in the driving coils, which would negate the speed advantages of the memristive processor. A significant challenge identified during simulation is the "sneak path" current in the crossbar array, which introduces non-linearity in the VMM. However, the adaptive nature of the LMS algorithm proved robust enough to treat these non-linearities as part of the plant dynamics, automatically adjusting the weights to compensate.

6. Conclusion

This paper presented the design and theoretical validation of an Analog Neuromorphic Hydraulic Governor. By integrating memristive in-memory computing with high-speed piezoelectric actuation, we demonstrated a novel approach to hydraulic transient suppression that operates entirely in the analog domain. The system leverages the parallelism of physics—specifically Ohm's Law and Kirchhoff's Laws—to perform complex adaptive filtering with nanosecond latency. These findings suggest that analog neuromorphic architectures are not merely alternatives to digital computing but are superior candidates for ultra-low-latency control systems in critical infrastructure. Future work will focus on the hardware fabrication of the $Ta/HfO_2$ crossbar and experimental validation in a high-pressure test rig.
